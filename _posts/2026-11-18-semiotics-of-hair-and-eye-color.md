In Western animation, a character's hair and eye colors primarily serve to create a realistic appearance or an easily recognizable silhouette. In anime, the palette functions differently: it operates as a full-fledged sign system rooted in Japanese Kabuki theater, Shinto symbolism, and the history of book illustration. An attentive viewer can decode a character's temperament, social status, and even tragic narrative fate within seconds of their first appearance on screen.

### 1. White and Silver: The Mark of the Otherworldly and Tragedy

In Japanese culture, white (*shiro*) is traditionally associated not only with purity and sacredness, but also with death, mourning, and the spirit world. During the Edo period, white burial garments were worn by the deceased and by samurai preparing for ritual suicide.

Within the semiotics of anime, characters with white or silver hair almost invariably belong to one of three categories:

* **Supernatural beings and divine messengers.** Fox spirits (*kitsune*), deities, or entities of non-human origin—such as Tomoe in *Kamisama Kiss* or Ginko in *Mushishi*, whose hair turned white after an encounter with the supernatural.
* **Heroes with tragic fates or artificial origins.** Albinism and premature gray hair signify the fragility of life, cruel experimentation, or impending mortality (such as Kaori Miyazono in *Your Lie in April*, or Ken Kaneki in *Tokyo Ghoul*, whose hair turns white at the moment of psychological rupture).
* **Detached geniuses and the "kuudere" archetype.** Cool, pale tones underscore a character's emotional distance from the ordinary human world (such as Rei Ayanami in *Neon Genesis Evangelion*).

### 2. Red: Passion, Righteous Fury, and Protection Against Evil

Red (*aka*) is the most sacred color in Japan. Shinto shrine gates (*torii*) are painted vermilion because the pigment is believed to ward off evil spirits and disease. In Kabuki theater, bold red lines of stage makeup (*kumadori*) signify noble rage, righteous indignation, and superhuman strength.

In character design, red hair and eyes convey two primary semantic layers:

* **Unbridled energy and directness.** Fiery-haired protagonists rarely engage in intrigue; they act impulsively, protect the weak, and possess an acute sense of justice (such as Tanjiro Kamado in *Demon Slayer* or Shoyo Hinata in *Haikyu!!*).
* **Ties to mystical power or a curse.** Crimson eyes paired with dark hair serve as a classic marker of ancient lineage, demonic blood, or forbidden abilities (the Kurta clan in *Hunter × Hunter*, or Itachi Uchiha in *Naruto*).

### 3. Blue: Melancholy, Intellect, and Water

Historically, the Japanese color term *ao* encompassed both blue and green spectra, symbolizing water, tranquility, and depth. In modern animation, the blue palette stands in binary opposition to red—just as intellect contrasts with emotion.

* **Cool rationality and introversion.** Blue-haired characters frequently serve as strategists, observers, or quiet introverts concealing deep feelings beneath a calm exterior (Ami Mizuno in *Sailor Moon*, Megumi Fushiguro in *Jujutsu Kaisen*).
* **Hidden sorrow and self-sacrifice.** In dramatic narratives, cool blue tones amplify a character's sense of solitude and alienation from a noisy social world.

Crucially, contemporary directors often play on audience expectations by deliberately subverting these established codes. When a character with "angelic" pale hair turns out to be the central antagonist—such as Griffith in *Berserk* or Johan Liebert in *Monster*—the artistic impact relies precisely on the sharp collision between the visual sign of purity and the hero's inner void.
