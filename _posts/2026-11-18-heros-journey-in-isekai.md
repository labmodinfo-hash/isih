The *isekai* genre (literally "another world"), in which an ordinary student or office worker is suddenly transported into a fantasy universe, is frequently criticized for its formulaic plots and escapism. However, from the perspective of structural anthropology and comparative mythology, modern portal fantasy narratives are not merely products of mass culture—they are precise reconstructions of humanity's oldest narrative pattern: the rite of passage and the "monomyth" described by American mythologist Joseph Campbell.

### 1. Symbolic Death as Crossing the Threshold

In traditional cultures, initiation rites—marking an adolescent's transition into adulthood—consistently followed a three-part structure formulated by ethnographer Arnold van Gennep: separation, liminality (the threshold state), and incorporation.

In classic *isekai*, the separation stage is taken to an absolute extreme: the protagonist literally dies in the familiar world (the well-known "truck-kun" trope) or is instantaneously torn away from everyday routine.

From a semiotic standpoint, the protagonist's death in the opening episode is not mere narrative cynicism, but a symbolic rejection of a former, "immature" status. To gain new agency and strength, the hero's old self must die.

### 2. The "Magic Gift" and Shifting Social Rules

In archaic myths and folktales—analyzed in detail by Vladimir Propp in *Morphology of the Folktale*—a hero who crosses the threshold into the otherworld inevitably encounters a Donor: a deity or sage who bestows a magical agent upon them.

In contemporary anime, this function is assumed by a summoning goddess or the world's own game-like "System":

* **Superpowers as compensation.** In the real world, the protagonist often feels invisible or marginalized. A unique skill ("cheat ability") granted upon transition acts as a mythological totem conferring the right to perform heroic deeds.
* **Transparent rules.** The game mechanics of the new world (levels, guilds, clear hierarchies of monsters) reduce existential anxiety. Unlike complex and unpredictable modern society, effort in the otherworld is always rewarded with proportional growth.

### 3. From Escapism to Archetypal Deconstruction

While early works in the genre strictly adhered to the heroic myth schema—where the protagonist defeats the Demon King and saves the world—recent anime has shifted toward deep deconstruction of this archetype:

* In *Re:Zero − Starting Life in Another World*, the creators deliberately strip the hero of overwhelming combat power, leaving him only the ability to rewind time through his own death. This transforms a fairytale adventure into a psychological study of trauma and hubris.
* In *KonoSuba: God's Blessing on This Wonderful World!*, the classical heroic epic is inverted through carnivalesque satire, pushing the archetypes of the "wise goddess" and the "noble knight" to absurdity.

Ultimately, the enduring popularity of *isekai* lies in its ability to offer modern audiences an experience scarce in the digital age: symbolic coming-of-age—a journey from a bewildered observer to an individual capable of taking responsibility for an entire world.
